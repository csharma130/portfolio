Personal Projects

P1
Line-Following RC Car

Built a 4-motor Arduino-based line-following robot. Configured power distribution across a microcontroller, L293D motor driver, and IR sensor array. Diagnosed and resolved hardware faults including wiring issues, voltage drop, and driver-library compatibility through systematic testing.

Components:

  • → DC Motors — hobbyist-recommended motors selected because the application only required simple rotational drive; no need for more complex servo or stepper motor control.
  • → L293D Motor Driver — capable of independently controlling all four DC motors, making it the right fit for a 4-motor drive configuration.
  • → Arduino Uno (Elegoo Dupe) — chosen over the official board for cost savings while retaining identical functionality for this application.
  • → IR Sensors — selected based on research confirming they were sufficient for line-following at this scale; a more expensive sensor offered no meaningful advantage for this use case.
  • → Cardboard Chassis — used as a resourceful first-iteration frame. A future iteration would use a PETG 3D printed chassis, as the cardboard sagged under the weight of the microcontroller and motor driver. PETG offers the rigidity needed to support the electronics while remaining lightweight and impact-resistant.

Key Engineering Challenges:

1. IR sensors were producing erratic, unstable readings that prevented consistent line detection. Wrote a dedicated test script to methodically evaluate each sensor individually, which allowed fine-tuning of sensitivity thresholds and precise sensor positioning. This process also revealed how to account for interference from ambient lighting and surface reflections, ensuring the sensors only responded to the intended line.

2. All four wheels were rotating in inconsistent directions. The root cause was improper motor wiring at the terminal blocks on the L293D motor driver. Rewiring the terminals to the correct configuration resolved the issue immediately and restored proper directional control across all four motors.

3. Motors were not receiving enough power to turn. The original power source did not account for the saturation voltage drop across the L293D motor driver, meaning the voltage available at the motors fell below the minimum needed for rotation. Increasing the supply voltage provided enough headroom to exceed the driver's saturation voltage, allowing the motors to operate correctly.

P2
4-DOF Robotic Arm

Building a 4 degree-of-freedom robotic arm to develop hands-on hardware design, embedded systems, and mechanical prototyping skills. Currently in the mechanical design phase.

Components:

  • → Elegoo Arduino Mega — chosen over the official Arduino for cost savings while retaining identical functionality for the application.
  • → SG90 & MG996R Servos — widely recommended hobbyist servos with well-documented torque and stall current specs, selected based on the torque requirements of each joint in the arm.
  • → 5V/10A Power Distribution Block — rated to handle the worst-case power draw, calculated by summing the stall currents of all servos simultaneously.
  • → 5V/10A Screw Terminal Barrel Jack Adapter — used to couple the AC wall adapter to the power distribution block, matched to the same voltage and current rating to avoid a bottleneck in the power chain.

Key Engineering Challenges:

1. Sourcing compatible power components required significant research. The servos needed a power distribution block rated for the correct amperage and voltage, which took time to identify. This also required a barrel jack adapter to step down wall AC power to the DC voltage the servos needed, and a screw terminal barrel jack adapter to properly couple the AC adapter to the power distribution block — each component had to be verified for compatibility before the system could be powered safely.

Professional Projects

01
New Process Recharacterization

Problem Statement: A newly launched spore disc production line produced 33 defective lots out of 102 within its first three months — a 32.4% defect rate. Defective lots were scrapped. The failure mode: one or more samples out of 12 per lot falling below 50% of the lot mean biological concentration.

What Was Done: Conducted a multi-year root cause investigation covering 20 distinct process variables, including impeller design, mixing speed, dispense rate, and positional instability, among others.

How?: Each variable was tested individually. Before running a test, I wrote out the full test protocol in a lab notebook with traceable, explicitly defined steps. After execution, samples were submitted to QA and the results were analyzed in Minitab to determine whether that variable had a statistically meaningful effect on the failure mode. Test preparation varied significantly depending on the variable being studied. Some required designing and fabricating positional fixtures via rapid prototyping; others involved collecting timed aliquots in test tubes at incremental intervals to observe process behavior over time. The investigation ultimately revealed that the failures stemmed from multiple root causes working in combination, not a single variable. That finding would not have surfaced without the disciplined, one-variable-at-a-time approach.

Results: Currently in the design transfer phase. Projecting $250K in annual cost savings upon completion.

02
Upgraded Autoclave Rack

Problem Statement: Bottles tipping inside the original rack design rendered one autoclave cycle non-operational, eliminating it from production use entirely.

What Was Done: Designed and built a new autoclave rack that resolved the tipping issue and restored full operability to the affected cycle. The new design also expanded compatibility across additional cycle configurations, increasing the rack's overall utility within the manufacturing environment.

How?: Started by measuring the existing rack dimensions using a high-fidelity ruler to define design constraints. A legacy rack, no longer available from the original manufacturer but still physically on hand, served as the reference. I used calipers to measure hole diameters and center-to-center spacing, documented the total hole count, and calculated available surface area to confirm adequate steam penetration in the new design. Everything was modeled in SolidWorks, with properly sized structural beams incorporated to address the beam deflection present in the legacy design. The finished design was sent to a third-party vendor for machining in stainless steel and then passivated to meet GMP material and cleanliness requirements. For verification, thermocouples were mounted at the same positions used during the original autoclave validation study, and the worst-case cycle was selected for testing: the one with the highest temperature, slowest exhaust time, and longest exposure duration. Temperatures at each location were confirmed to be within specification per the original validation protocol.

Results: Increased autoclave usage cases by 20% and eliminated beam deflection by 100%.

Senior Design Project

01
Neonatal Surfactant Delivery Device

Problem Statement: Physicians at UC Davis Medical Center found that existing tools for Less Invasive Surfactant Administration (LISA) were too rigid and hard to maneuver in the neonatal airway, causing unnecessary tracheal trauma.

What Was Done: Led development of a neonatal surfactant delivery catheter as part of the UC Davis Biomedical Engineering senior design program.

How?: The project started with a direct observation of the LISA procedure alongside a physician to understand the clinical pain points firsthand. From there, the team built a structured user needs survey, followed by a state-of-the-art review that benchmarked competing devices across cost, dimensions, materials, intended users, and market availability. After generating and narrowing down multiple design concepts, the team developed a prototype built around three physician-specified requirements: better maneuverability, depth visibility markers, and a softer durometer. Work spanned the full product development cycle, including material selection, manufacturing method evaluation, FMEA, and V&V testing. Since no ISO standard exists for intratracheal catheters specifically, testing requirements were adapted from ISO 10555 (intravascular catheters) as the closest applicable standard, which required deliberate engineering judgment in interpreting and applying those requirements to a different anatomical context.
